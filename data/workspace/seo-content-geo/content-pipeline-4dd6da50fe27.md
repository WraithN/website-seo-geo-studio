# Chinese Names for Boys: A Practical Guide for Foreigners

If you are a foreigner living in China, married into a Chinese family, or just curious about how Chinese boy names work, this guide is for you. I have spent years helping friends and colleagues pick Chinese names, and I have seen the same questions come up again and again: What does this character mean? Why does the surname come first? Will people actually be able to pronounce it?

This page walks through the logic behind Chinese boy names, gives you a working vocabulary for choosing one, and points you to deeper guides on pronunciation, surnames, and name lists.

## What makes a Chinese boy name different from a Western one

A Chinese name is usually three characters: a one-character surname followed by a one- or two-character given name. The surname comes first because Chinese naming tradition puts family before the individual [^1]. That single structural fact explains a lot of the confusion foreigners run into.

The given name is not just a label. Parents choose characters for meaning, sound, and sometimes for a generational poem that assigns one shared character to every child in the same generation [^2]. So a name can carry a family's hope for virtue, strength, or good fortune, and it can also mark where a person sits in the family tree.

## The building blocks: surname, generation name, given name

- **Surname (姓, xìng):** inherited, usually one character. A small number of two-character surnames exist.
- **Generation name (字辈, zìbèi):** optional shared character across siblings or cousins, common in some families and regions.
- **Given name (名, míng):** one or two characters chosen by the parents.

## How parents actually choose the characters

In my experience, most families weigh four things at once:

1. **Meaning.** Characters like 强 (qiáng, strength), 明 (míng, brightness), 志 (zhì, ambition), and 安 (ān, peace) are common because the meaning is easy to explain.
2. **Sound.** The full name should flow when spoken, and it should not sound like an unfortunate homophone. This is where a native speaker's ear matters.
3. **Five elements and fortune.** Some families consult a fortune teller or a name calculator that maps birth date to the five elements (五行, wǔxíng) and picks characters that balance them [^3].
4. **Family rules.** Some families forbid reusing a character from an ancestor's name, and some still follow a generation poem.

## A short list of common characters and what they signal

| Character | Pinyin | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 伟 | wěi | great, grand |
| 强 | qiáng | strong |
| 磊 | lěi | upright, open |
| 浩 | hào | vast |
| 宇 | yǔ | universe |
| 轩 | xuān | high, elegant |
| 睿 | ruì | wise |
| 泽 | zé | pool, grace |

This is a starting point, not a menu. The same character can read differently depending on the surname it sits next to.

## Pronunciation: the part most foreigners underestimate

Chinese names are written in pinyin, but pinyin is not English. The letters look familiar and the sounds are not. Tones change meaning, and a wrong tone can turn a dignified name into a joke. I always tell people to learn the four tones before they finalize a name, because you will be saying it every day [^4].

## Mistakes I see foreigners make

- Picking a name that sounds like a Western name but means something odd in Chinese.
- Ignoring the surname when testing the full name out loud.
- Choosing a character that is rare or hard to write, which makes paperwork painful.
- Assuming the given name is the family name when filling out forms.
